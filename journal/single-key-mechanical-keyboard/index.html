What is a single-key mechanical keyboard?

Shop Notes, from the Key Bored workshop · October 18, 2026 · 4 min read

A single-key mechanical keyboard is one real keyboard switch and one keycap, mounted in a small body you can hold. Most of them don't plug into anything. You press it because pressing it feels good, and that turns out to be reason enough.

A keyboard with everything removed except the good part

Ask someone who builds custom keyboards what they like about them and you'll hear about sound and feel long before you hear about typing speed. They talk about the thock of a heavy board, the scratch of a cheap switch, and the way a key bottoms out on a stiff plate. It's a hobby built around the half second your finger spends going down and coming back up.

A single-key keyboard keeps that half second and throws away the other 80-odd keys. What's left is small enough to sit next to a mouse or ride in a pocket, and it gets pressed constantly, mostly without the owner noticing.

What's inside

There are three parts, and each one changes how the key feels.

The switch is the mechanism. It has a stem that slides inside a housing, a spring that pushes it back up, and on some switches a leaf or click bar that adds a bump or a sound. Most switches people talk about follow the MX design: about 4 mm of total travel, with the press registering roughly halfway down.

The keycap is what your finger touches. Its shape (the profile), its plastic, and its wall thickness all change the sound. A thin, light cap sounds higher and clackier than a thick one.

The body holds the switch, and this is where most single-key gadgets cut corners. An MX-style switch is designed to clip into a 14 mm square hole in a plate 1.5 mm thick. Hold it at that thickness and it locks in tight. Make the plate thinner or the hole sloppy and the switch rattles, and every press sounds a little hollow.

Why anyone wants one

Some people use them the way others click pens: on calls, while reading, while thinking through a problem. Others treat them as desk objects, in the same category as a good pen or a small brass paperweight. Plenty of keyboard people buy one so they can try a switch without building a whole board around it.

The idea isn't new, and it's been a joke before. In 2021 Stack Overflow took an April Fools' prank about a keyboard with only copy and paste keys and actually shipped it. People bought it. Small keyboards that do one thing well have a strange pull.

Where the cheap ones go wrong

Most inexpensive single-key clickers are thin 3D-printed shells. They look fine in photos. In the hand they feel light and slightly loose, because printed plastic rarely holds the 14 mm opening and 1.5 mm plate exactly, and because a thin shell rings when the switch bottoms out.

We learned how small the margins are while building our own. On printed prototypes, a tenth of a millimeter in the switch opening is the difference between a switch that snaps in with a click and one that falls out when you turn the key over. Our print files now carry different openings for different printing processes for that reason.

How we approached ours

The KB OneKey is one solid piece of colored polycarbonate. The switch clips straight into the body at the standard plate thickness, and underneath it there's an open air chamber. We tuned that chamber for a crisper sound, closer to a key on a full board than a key in a box.

The KB OneKey Pro goes the other way. It slides a solid brass weight up into an anodized aluminum case and holds it with four Torx screws threaded straight into the brass. The extra mass lowers the sound and makes the key feel planted. It weighs about three times as much as the plastic one, and you notice it the moment you pick it up.

How to pick one

Start with the switch, because it decides most of the experience. If you share a room, pick something quiet. If you work alone and want feedback, a clicky switch is very satisfying. Our guide to linear, tactile, and clicky switches goes deeper.

Then think about weight. Light keys are easy to carry. Heavy ones sound deeper and stay put on a desk. And check that the switch is hot-swappable, which means you can pull it out and try another without soldering. You'll want to, eventually. Everyone does.

Quick answers

Does a single-key keyboard type?

Usually not. Most, including OneKey, aren't connected to a computer. They're made for the press, the sound, and the feel.

Can you change the switch?

On OneKey, yes. It takes standard MX-style switches, so you can try linear, tactile, or clicky switches in the same body.

Is it loud?

That depends on the switch. Linear and tactile switches are moderate, clicky switches are the loudest, and silent switches are built to be quiet.

What makes one feel premium?

A body that holds the switch tightly at the standard plate thickness, a good keycap, and enough mass that it doesn't feel hollow.

How small-batch pre-orders work

Shop Notes, from the Key Bored workshop · October 18, 2026 · 2 min read

A pre-order means you pay for something before it's made. Small makers use pre-orders to build exactly the quantity people want instead of guessing, which lets them spend money on quality rather than on unsold stock. You get a better object. The trade-off is that you wait for it.

  2. Where the model comes from

Why makers do it this way

Precision parts are expensive to set up and cheap to repeat. A machine shop spends real time programming a part, fixturing it, and checking the first one. After that, each additional part costs much less. That pushes small makers toward ordering in batches.

Without pre-orders, a maker has to guess how many to make. Guess high and there's a warehouse full of unsold product. Guess low and people who wanted one miss out. Pre-orders replace the guess with a number.

Where the model comes from

The keyboard hobby runs on this. Enthusiasts have used group buys for years: an interest check to see if people want a design, then an order window, then months of production. Some of the most coveted keycap sets in the hobby only exist because enough people committed up front.

The hobby also knows the downside. Group buys are famous for delays, and anyone who's waited eight months for keycaps has opinions about communication. Good makers learned that updates matter as much as the product.

What the timeline looks like

A typical pre-order runs in stages. There's an order window. When it closes, parts are ordered in the exact quantities sold. Then production, then quality checks, then shipping in batches.

Every date before the end is an estimate. A good maker gives you an honest estimate, tells you when it moves, and tells you why. In the US, the FTC's mail order rule also requires sellers to ship within the time they state, or within 30 days if they don't state one, and to notify you and offer a refund if they can't.

Numbered batches

Some makers number each production run. A numbered batch is made once and never remade, so the early units become a small piece of the product's history.

OneKey launches with Batch 001, 500 numbered units. The NFC tag in each one opens a page that shows its number, so ownership is something you can tap and show rather than just claim.

What to check before you buy

Look for a clear ship estimate, a plain cancellation policy, and real photos of real prototypes rather than renders alone. Check whether the maker explains what happens if something goes wrong.

Here's ours, in short: you're charged at checkout, you can cancel for a full refund any time before production starts, and if the date moves we email everyone with the new timeline and the reason. The full version is on our pre-order page.
